The Night of All Nights

The Full Moon of Kārtik

Of all the full moons in the year, the full moon of the month of Kārtik — called Śārad Pūrṇimā, the full moon of Autumn — shines with a special brilliance. The air is clear after the monsoons have passed, and the moon rises larger and brighter than on any other night.

In the Vedic tradition, this full moon is not simply an astronomical event. The Srimad Bhagavatam describes how Lord Krishna, hearing this very moon rise over Vrindavan, lifted His flute to His lips and played a song that only the gopis could hear in their hearts — a call that drew them through forests and across fields to the banks of the Yamuna.

"On that autumn night, the moon rose, cooling the forest with its rays like the face of Lakshmidevi. The lotuses bloomed as if smiling. The Lord, seeing the forest lit by the moon's glory, chose to perform His most intimate pastime — the Rāsa dance."

— Śrīmad Bhāgavatam 10.29.1–3

The Supreme Pastime

The Rāsa Līlā of Vrindavan

When Krishna played His flute on the night of Sharad Purnima, the gopis of Vrindavan experienced a longing unlike any worldly desire. They left everything — their homes, their families, their duties — and ran through the moonlit forest toward the Yamuna.

Krishna, to test the purity of their love, first sent them back. He told them their duties lay at home. But the gopis replied with a wisdom born of pure devotion: "Our only duty is to You. All dharma finds its fulfilment at Your lotus feet."

Then the dance began. Krishna expanded Himself so that each gopi danced with Him alone — feeling His hand in hers, His glance meant only for her. The dance lasted through the entire night, yet when it ended, the gopis felt it had passed in a single breath.

"The Supreme Lord, who removes the distress of those who surrender to Him, danced with the gopis in the same way a child plays with his reflection in a mirror."

— Śrīmad Bhāgavatam 10.33.3

The Rāsa Līlā is not a romantic story in the worldly sense. It is the supreme teaching on selfless love — bhakti in its most complete form. The gopis represent the soul longing for union with God, and their dance is the soul's highest fulfillment.

The Sacred Tradition

Moonlit Kheer — Amrita from the Sky

On the night of Sharad Purnima, the moon is said to pour amrita — divine nectar — along with its rays. The ancient tradition is to prepare kheer (rice pudding cooked in milk and sugar) and place it outdoors under the open sky for the entire night, so the moonlight can infuse it with this nectar.

In the morning, this kheer is offered to Krishna and distributed as prasadam to all devotees. Many believe it carries healing properties and spiritual merit for those who receive it with faith.

  • Kheer is prepared fresh on the day of Purnima with milk, rice, sugar, and saffron
  • Offered to the Lord at sunset with prayers and kirtan
  • Placed under the open sky through the night, bathed in moonlight
  • Distributed as prasadam to all in the morning after the morning program
🍚

"He who eats the kheer kept in moonlight on this night is freed from ill health for the entire year."

— Traditional belief recorded in Ayurvedic texts

At ISKCON Coimbatore

How We Celebrate

At Sri Jagannath Mandir, Coimbatore, Sharad Purnima is celebrated across the full day and into the moonlit night. Everyone is welcome.

🌅

Morning — Mangala Arati & Special Abhishek

Special deity bath (abhishek) with panchamrita, flowers and milk. The morning program begins before dawn.

📖

Afternoon — Bhagavatam Class on the Rāsa Līlā

A deep class on the 10th Canto of Srimad Bhagavatam — the chapters describing Krishna's most confidential pastime.

🪔

Evening — Grand Moonrise Kirtan

As the full moon rises, devotees sing in continuous kirtan through the night. Lamps are lit and the temple is decorated with white flowers.

🍚

Night — Moonlit Kheer & Prasadam Feast

The kheer offered to the Lord is kept under the open sky all night. A full feast prasadam is distributed to all present.
